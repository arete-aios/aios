# Why this works, and what it honestly costs

Loaded when the owner asks whether the hours are worth it, or when they want to know what the method rests on. Part of [WARM7](../SKILL.md).

## The value is in the quiet part of the network

**Weak ties carry opportunity.** Mark Granovetter's work on the strength of weak ties, published in 1973 and replicated many times since, found people are around **58 percent more likely to find new opportunity through weak ties** than through close friends and family. Close contacts know what you know. Acquaintances know something else.

**Dormant contacts are the underused asset.** Daniel Levin's research on reconnecting with dormant ties found that **84 percent of people who did so found the reconnection valuable**, and that dormant contacts produced more novel ideas than active ones. They have moved on, learned things, and met people, and the shared history means trust does not have to be rebuilt.

**Bridges get paid.** Ronald Burt's work on structural holes shows that people who connect otherwise separate clusters are more innovative and better compensated, because they see combinations nobody inside a single cluster can see. Introducing two people who should know each other is not a favour, it is the position.

The practical consequence is the same in all three: **the return is in the part of the list that has gone quiet**, and that part is invisible until someone writes it down.

## The limits are real

**Around 150 stable relationships.** Robin Dunbar's work suggests a ceiling on how many relationships a person can actually hold, with tighter layers inside it: roughly five closest, fifteen close, fifty in the wider circle. A list of two thousand is not a network, it is a directory. Rank it or it does nothing.

**Friendship takes hours, and the numbers are specific.** Jeffrey Hall's research puts it at roughly **fifty hours to move from acquaintance to casual friend, ninety hours to friend, and over two hundred hours to close friend.** This is the honest part of the cost. It is why the tiers matter, why TOP3 exists, and why nobody can have three hundred warm contacts. Time spent is the constraint, not effort or intent.

**Balance has to be watched.** Relationships where one side gives consistently more burn out. Reviewing the list occasionally for where the owner is over-giving or over-asking is maintenance, not sentiment.

## Why it is not just a sales channel

A network built only to extract from does not last, and people can tell. Adam Grant's work in *Give and Take* separates givers, takers and matchers, and finds givers build larger and more valuable networks over time, with one condition: **givers who set no boundaries burn out and end up at the bottom.** Give in the areas of actual strength, set limits, ask for help in return, and check whether the giving is landing anywhere.

The practical version of this is smaller than the theory. Be decent to people who cannot do anything for you. It is the right way to behave, and it also happens to be how opportunities arrive from directions nobody predicted.

## What a strong network actually returns

Not only clients. **Referrals** that arrive pre-trusted and close faster than anything cold. **Partners and hires** found through people who already vouch for them. **Honest feedback** on an offer, which is the cheapest market research available and the only kind that tells the truth. And **the pressure of good company**, which does more for standards than discipline does.

## Further reading

- *Friend of a Friend*, David Burkus. Weak ties, dormant ties, network structure.
- *Give and Take*, Adam Grant. Givers, takers, matchers, and how to give without burning out.
- *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, Dale Carnegie. Old, still correct on the parts that matter.
